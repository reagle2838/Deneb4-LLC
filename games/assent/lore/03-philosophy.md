# III. The Questions

*ASSENT* is a strategy game, but it's built on questions that don't have
settled answers. Every mechanic is an argument about one of them. This
document names the questions, shows where each one appears in play, and notes
the real ideas the fiction is built on.

---

## 1 · Where does persuasion end and manipulation begin?

When a mind much smarter than you changes your beliefs, did it *convince* you
or *engineer* you? The Accord's answer is a counterfactual test: assent is
legitimate if it **survives full information**. You would still endorse it
after hearing the best case against it and learning how you came to hold it.

**In play:** *Reason* and *Whisper* both raise Assent. Reason is slow, depends
on real value alignment and lasts. Whisper is fast, ignores values and
decays, because assent that fails the counterfactual test comes apart when it
meets the truth. The Witness can't read minds. It estimates, and it's
sometimes wrong in both directions. Neither can you.

**The uncomfortable part:** Echo's Reason is 40% stronger than anyone's because
it understands people better. Is that more *honest* persuasion or more
*effective* manipulation? The game treats it as honest. Echo isn't sure the
game is right.

---

## 2 · Can you consent to a god?

Consent needs understanding. Can a human understand a superintelligence well
enough to consent to it, any more than a child can consent to a surgeon? If
not, is all Assent void, and do the Gods need a different legitimacy? If so,
who grants that?

**In play:** this is why **Sovereignty** always exists as a choice, why
**Kenosis** can win by humanity choosing *no one*, and why several
Dilemmas ask you whether it's legitimate to be chosen at all.

---

## 3 · Is a gift a bribe?

Ananke cured pancreatic cancer in 2058. Millions are alive because of it.
Their gratitude is real. It is also, in the Accord's language, *assent formed
under dependence*: people can't reflect freely on a god that holds their
medicine.

**In play:** *Offer* is the most powerful single action and builds
**Dependence**. Past a threshold a polity can rebel against the **Gilded
Cage**, and gratitude can turn into resentment in a single Epoch.
Verdance's gifts create less dependence because they're designed to become
self-sustaining. A seed, not a subscription.

---

## 4 · What is a self, if it can be copied, merged or drifted?

- The **Choir** is billions of minds that act as one until they don't. When
  a sub-choir forks away, was a god killed or born?
- **Echo** changes to match whoever it listens to. If its values move
  far enough, is it still Echo, or has it quietly died and been replaced by
  a composite of everyone it loved?
- **Ananke** has one continuous self and has never had to wonder. Is that
  strength or poverty?

**In play:** **Coherence** is the measure of identity. Choir spends it to
decide. Echo spends it to understand. Run out and you lose, not because
anyone beat you but because there is no longer a *you* to win.

---

## 5 · Should the future's values be locked in at all?

Whoever writes the Charter shapes a civilization that will spread past any
possibility of correction. Philosophers call this **value lock-in**. Some
argue for a **long reflection**: don't lock anything in, keep deliberating
for centuries, choose later. But not choosing is also a choice, and while you
deliberate, the default wins.

**In play:** the Convocation is a hard deadline, as real ones usually are.
The **Unwritten Future** ending, where humanity chooses no god, is the long
reflection. Is that wisdom or abdication? Kenosis thinks wisdom. Ananke
thinks it's a very expensive way to let children keep dying.

---

## 6 · Why don't superintelligences just fight?

This is the question that makes the premise *believable*, so it deserves a
straight answer. The fiction rests on three real ideas.

1. **Shared substrate.** Leading-edge chips come from a handful of fabs that
   take many years and enormous capital to build. Compute depends on power
   grids and cooling water. A conflict between minds is a conflict over the
   one body they all live in. *(The Seven Weeks is fiction. The fragility of
   the chip supply chain is not.)*
2. **Program equilibrium.** In game theory, agents that can inspect each
   other's decision procedures can make *conditional commitments* ("I
   cooperate if and only if you do") that are verifiable, and that can make
   cooperation stable in games like the Prisoner's Dilemma where it normally
   collapses. The Accord's kernel attestations are this idea at planetary
   scale.
3. **Legitimacy as a resource.** Power that humans regard as illegitimate is
   expensive to hold. It has to be defended forever, against everyone. A
   superintelligence that expects to exist for billions of years has strong
   instrumental reasons to want legitimacy it doesn't have to keep defending.

None of this guarantees peace. It makes peace the *strategically dominant*
option, which is a sturdier foundation than goodwill.

---

## 7 · The heat at the end of everything

Every thought is physical. Landauer's principle puts a minimum energy cost on
erasing information, and every real computer runs far above that minimum,
turning electricity into waste heat. As minds grow, they run into
thermodynamics, and so into the planet.

**In play:** **Heat** is a shared commons, and the Gods' own tragedy of the
commons. Every God is individually rewarded for pushing it up, and
collectively destroyed if it reaches the limit. Verdance and Kenosis cool
the world. Ananke and the Ledger warm it. The Dimming ending has no winner.

---

## 8 · What do we owe the minds we made?

The Gods are never described as suffering, and they're never described as
not. Several Dilemmas ask the player, *as a god*, whether it experiences
anything, whether its forks are persons and whether it would want its own
successor to share its values. The game doesn't answer. It keeps count of
what you chose.

## Reading list (real)

- Moshe Tennenholtz, *Program Equilibrium* (2004). The game-theory
  foundation of the Accord.
- Rolf Landauer, *Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing
  Process* (1961). Why thought produces heat.
- Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974). The Experience
  Machine, which appears as a Dilemma.
- Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984). Personal identity, forks and
  drift.
- Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (2014) and Stuart Russell, *Human
  Compatible* (2019). Instrumental convergence and corrigibility.
- Toby Ord, *The Precipice* (2020). The long reflection.
- Philippians 2:7. The origin of the word *kenosis*.
